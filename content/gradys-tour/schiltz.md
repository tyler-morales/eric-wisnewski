Napoli - Italy

Sitting at the hostel, I wait for the newest addition to a long cast of characters on this trip: Schiltz. He had a first name once, but it has been long lost to history. It’s only to be summoned in whispers by those who knew him long.. long ago or government officials.



At first glance Schiltz is the everyman. He is of average height, works 9-5 and pays his taxes. He is humble, reflective and good-hearted. But he does have one special almost supernatural ability, his approachability. I’ve seen complete strangers do and say things to him that would leave me reeling. I’ve witnessed him accidentally have dinner with a pimp, make clothing deals with a homeless crackhead and be catcalled aggressively by seemingly nice women. Even the Japanese, a people who take painstaking efforts to not even sit next to a foreigner on a crowded train, could not resist approaching him. During a recent trip with him to Japan, I witnessed the following: an elderly woman in a sushi restaurant smiling at him and in broken English called him ‘handsome boy’; in back-to-back bars he was compared first to Tom Hanks, then to Tom Horr[ll]and; and to top it all off, in a bathhouse full of stark-naked Japanese men, he received affectionate jeers and thigh slaps. A magnetic object of desire, complete strangers of all ages feel fully free to harass, grab and say all manner of things to him. It’s a unique and endearing quality.

![](/images/uploads/IMG_0864.jpeg)

*Schiltz under question in Japan, for eating ice cream.*

Now, he is nearly here in Napoli. He **needed** this break. A few years back, he gave up a life of soulless profiteering in high finance to dedicate his time to search and rescue operations and management in Texas. He even says “y’all” now. 

I eagerly wait in the hostel, **but he never shows.** The storm cloud that follows him and everything he touches next is staggering. 

I receive a chain of alarming messages that there has been ‘a change of plan’, he is ‘on his way to the ER’ and ‘he may lose his eye’.

How did we get here? 

*Schiltz in his own words.*

![](/images/uploads/IMG_2607-2.jpeg)

> The plan was very simple. Take a 5-hour train down to Naples, meet up with Grady and T-Mo, then immediately get after it. On the train, my contacts were acting up, so I went to the bathroom to take them out. But as soon as I took out my right contact, I felt a searing pain erupt from my eye. I blinked a bunch, but after a few frantic seconds, the pain and blurriness intensified and a milky white haze began covering my vision. The tears began to come, but I wasn’t sad.
>
>
>
> Naturally, I went to Google to see if this was something serious, and its little AI summary gave me a cheery piece of advice: “you need to find a hospital immediately”.
>
>
>
> I had to come up with a plan fast. After talking to Grady, my parents, and an eye doctor I know from Chicago, I rushed off the train in Rome to call a taxi to the hospital. Taxis were getting scooped up left and right, and my Uber app essentially told me to go F myself because there was too much traffic for the app to keep up with.
>
>
>
> I started to have heart palpitations from the noise, commotion, and my increasingly worsening eye condition. Then some random man yelled at me, asking me if I needed a taxi. Did I “have cash?” I said no, so guided me to an ATM and told me he could take me to the hospital for 40 euros. Definitely a ripoff, but to skip the line, I agreed. I took out the cash, thinking that he was going to drive me, but then this big, lumbering elderly man pops out of nowhere next to him. “He will take you”. We walk to his beat-up Fiat, and he proceeds to text and drive the entire way without using a turn signal. He lets me know HE had to get eye surgery from the same place. We finally pull up to the eye hospital, and he hits me with a crisp “good luck” in Italian, takes the money and peels off.
>
>
>
> After paying an upfront $600, the hospital saw me pretty quickly. I learn that if I had not gone directly to the hospital and gotten this checked out, in the words of my Italian doctor, **“you have no more your eye”.**

The next morning, I debrief with T-Mo.

>  “Schiltz is done. He’s on the next flight back to Austin” - T-Mo

I call quickly and manage to talk him off the ledge, we head off to Rome to extract. We pick him up then grab a rental car, then head to the hills for Schiltz to recover. Like an aging rock star withdrawing from benzos, he must wear sunglasses day and night to protect the eye. He is clearly in a lot of pain and his humiliation continues as he walks glasses-first into a sliding door checking into the hotel. 

![](/images/uploads/IMG_2831.jpeg)

*Rescuing Schiltz from Rome*

A couple days after his ER visit the eye is on the mend, and we can finally start having some fun, so we drive to the beautiful town of Polignano a Mare.

![](/images/uploads/IMG_2829.jpeg)

*Polignano a Mare*

We sit down to dinner and Schiltz utters his famous last words:

> "Finally, I’m healthy and we can just sit down have a nice dinner and enjoy a couple days on the beach”.

For dinner we specifically look for a restaurant with my favorite dish, Spaghetti Frutti di Mare, and all order it. I can’t believe I’ve been in Italy this long and haven’t had it. We enjoy a couple bottles of wine, appetizers, and the food is great. But an hour later something starts to feel off. I excuse myself early and head to the apartment. By the time I lie in bed, I’m burning up and bracing myself for a long night. 2 hours into profuse sweating and fever dreams, I hear screaming from the bathroom.

> “Oh god. Oh my god. It’s coming.”

Out of my delirium, I piece together that Schiltz has been hit and is dealing with something very unpleasant in the bathroom. Rapidly, I realize I have urgent business in there as well. I pull myself up stumbling towards the can. I knock the door open, I see Schiltz seated with a look of despair and know I will have a long wait. I turn and feel something twist in my skull. The last thing I remember is pushing T-Mo’s door open before coming to in a heap on the ground. What I see next is surreal. T-Mo is standing over me screaming my name, and a stark-naked Schiltz is crouched inches away from me with a hand on my shoulder, telling me he’s calling an ambulance.

> “No don’t.. just wait.. I’m fine” 

I mutter, collapsed in my underwear on the hall floor. They tell me I passed out, slamming my head on the door, and, forever glass half full, I say chuckling:

> “Well, at least I didn’t shit myself”.

The silence that comes next is vast. I look up at Schiltz, he looks away, his sorrowful stare telling me enough.

I lie on the ground for the next ten minutes, unable to summon the strength to move, as T-Mo heads off for water and medicine. Eventually with all my strength, I clean up, and Schiltz and I lie on the cold ground together. 20 minutes go by, and his screams return as he runs to the bathroom, followed by crashing inside, “whatever you do don’t come in here”.



T-Mo returns with medicine and water, our only remaining healthy member. “Jesus, look at you two!” He says in shock and disgust. Like soldiers wounded in battle  we console each other before T-Mo begins to cough go pale then books it to the bathroom, where he starts to heave. We learned later he sent one off our third story down to the sidewalk. We are three men down with one bathroom, the next 8 hours are long ones.



We spend the next two days holed up in different accommodations drinking soup broth and eating as much rice and bread as we can stomach. 

![](/images/uploads/IMG_2825-1.jpeg)

![](/images/uploads/IMG_2826.jpeg)

*We recover im McDonalds and nap in a local park, killing time before our next checkin.*

Regaining some strength we reach my Grandmas cousins in Puglia only to find out the trains are cancelled Schiltz must leave immediately to make his flight they offer to drive him. Just like that, he is swept away off to a three-leg flight home. He texts that my cousins drive like Tokyo Drift, but they got him there in time. Strangely, two hours later we still haven’t heard from the cousins, even though it was only 15-minute drive. When we finally do we learn they were in a car crash right after dropping him off and something about everyone is alright but someone is in the hospital, but the Italian is too fast and I can’t follow. The storm cloud keeps following. We hear from him the next morning: he pulled an all-nighter only to have his flight canceled. This is followed by what can only be described as a full mental breakdown and a proclamation he will not be going to Europe for a “long, long, time”.

Thanks for reading my euro trip! 

Grady
